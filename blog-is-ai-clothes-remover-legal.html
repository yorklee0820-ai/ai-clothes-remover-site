Is It Legal to Remove Clothes from a Photo?

Consent, copyright and deepfake rules by region — what is allowed when you edit clothes in a photo, and what is not.

Editing clothes in a photo is legal in most places — but the legality depends on three things: whose photo it is, who is in it, and what the result looks like.

1. Copyright in the photo

Start with the image itself. If you took it, you generally own it. If someone else did, you need permission — a licence, a client agreement, or a stock licence that permits modification. Editing does not transfer ownership, and “I changed it substantially” is not a defence in most jurisdictions.

2. Consent and personality rights

The person in the photo matters more than the photo. Using someone's likeness for commercial purposes generally requires a model release. Publishing an altered image of a person — particularly one that is sexualised or misleading — can breach personality rights, and in some US states specific deepfake and digital-replica laws, regardless of whether money changed hands.

3. What the output depicts

This is where most of the legal exposure sits. Images of minors are prohibited outright in effectively every jurisdiction. Non-consensual intimate imagery is criminal in the UK, most US states, and across the EU. Several regions now also require AI-generated or AI-altered content to be labelled — which is why outputs from this tool carry an invisible watermark.

Practical checklist

  • You own the photo or have written permission to edit it.
  • Everyone depicted is an adult and has consented.
  • The result is non-sexual and does not misrepresent the person.
  • Commercial use is covered by a model release.
  • The image is labelled as AI-edited where the platform requires it.

This is general information, not legal advice. If the image is going into advertising or anything high-stakes, ask a lawyer in the relevant jurisdiction.
